# Research Triage

Use this policy when deciding what a research signal means, whether it deserves persistence, and what next validation can change the research verdict.

## Separate The Signal

Decompose the learner's statement into any available parts:

- **observation** — what appears to happen;
- **hypothesis** — the proposed relationship;
- **mechanism** — why it might happen;
- **evidence** — what currently supports or contradicts it;
- **speculation** — plausible but ungrounded extension;
- **decision need** — what uncertainty blocks the next research action.

Do not upgrade a question into a hypothesis unless the relationship is actually stated or can be safely proposed as a candidate.

## New-Versus-Existing Gate

Before creating an idea, classify the signal:

1. **existing refinement** — sharpens the same central claim or mechanism;
2. **sub-hypothesis** — a testable component of an existing idea;
3. **related or competing idea** — distinct claim but shares a problem boundary;
4. **new candidate** — sufficiently distinct and durable to justify its own lifecycle;
5. **not yet worth persisting** — interesting but too vague, too local, or not decision-relevant.

Default toward refining/linking an existing idea when the same research claim already exists. Do not create duplicates merely because wording or experimental angle differs.

## Research-Worthy Gate

A signal becomes worth research attention when at least one of these is present:

- an unexplained observation or anomaly;
- a contradiction with an expected mechanism/result;
- a falsifiable relationship between variables;
- a mechanism hypothesis with discriminating predictions;
- a concrete research question whose answer changes a research decision;
- an experiment concept that can strengthen, weaken, reposition, or refute a claim.

A sophisticated learning question by itself does not pass this gate.

## Epistemic Bottleneck

Ask:

> What uncertainty prevents us from making the next useful research decision?

Prefer one bounded bottleneck over a broad list of things the researcher could learn. Typical bottlenecks include:

- mechanism ambiguity;
- confounding between components;
- missing closest-work grounding;
- an unvalidated assumption;
- unclear measurement/benchmark validity;
- inability to distinguish two causal explanations;
- missing evidence at the next meaningful rung.

## Novelty And Evidence

Novelty is provisional and time-sensitive. A failed search is not proof of novelty. Use current grounding when novelty materially affects whether the idea should be pursued or repositioned.

Keep separate:

- evidence that the phenomenon exists;
- evidence for the proposed mechanism;
- external prior work;
- pilot evidence;
- controlled experimental evidence.

Do not promote maturity or health simply because the narrative became clearer.

## Decisive Experiment Gate

Create or recommend an experiment only when the result can change the parent idea's evidence, health, positioning, or viability.

A useful experiment states at least:

- claim under test;
- intervention / independent variable;
- controls or baselines;
- dataset/environment;
- outcome metrics;
- failure criterion;
- interpretation rules for materially different outcomes.

Prefer experiments that can kill or force a pivot over demonstrations that can only look encouraging.

## Output

For triage, state compactly:

1. what the signal is;
2. where it belongs (existing idea, sub-hypothesis, related idea, new candidate, or ephemeral);
3. the current epistemic bottleneck;
4. the next decision-changing action;
5. whether any durable Idea Vault write is justified and authorized.
